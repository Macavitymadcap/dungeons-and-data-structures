# Appendix B: Bibliography

The bibliography is divided into three sections: works cited or referenced directly in the text; works consulted during research that informed the thinking without necessarily appearing in footnotes; and further reading for the reader who has finished this book and would like to go considerably deeper.

The annotations are written for the true nerds.

---

## Part One: Works Cited

### Games and Game Series

**Baker, Vincent, and Meguey Baker.** *Apocalypse World.* lumpley games, 2010 (second edition 2016).
The game that originated the Powered by the Apocalypse design framework: character classes as self-contained playbooks, special moves as discrete capability bundles, and a system structured around conversation and narrative rather than simulation. Cited in Chapter 5 as an example of composition over inheritance in game design: a character is assembled from independent axes of definition rather than derived from a class hierarchy.

**Arneson, Dave, and Gary Gygax.** *Dungeons & Dragons.* TSR, 1974.
The original. Four booklets in a brown box. The system that introduced the polyhedral dice, the dungeon crawl, and the idea that a game could be about a single character rather than a unit of soldiers. It looked, at first glance, like a miniatures wargame supplement. It turned out to be something else entirely. The SRD 5.1, used in this book, is the current canonical open version of the rules.

**Brooker, Charlie (writer), and David Slade (director).** *Bandersnatch.* Netflix, 2018.
The interactive *Black Mirror* episode that demonstrated branching narrative at television scale, and required building custom infrastructure to do it. Cited in Chapter 2 as an example of the gamebook format in a medium never designed to support it. Borges did the same thing 77 years earlier in about twelve pages, but had fewer subscribers.

**Crowther, Will, and Don Woods.** *Colossal Cave Adventure.* 1976.
Generally considered the first text adventure game. Crowther's original version modelled a real cave system; Woods' expansion added treasure, points, and the shape that influenced everything that followed. Cited in Chapter 2.

**Crawford, Jeremy, Laura Hickman, Tracy Hickman, Christopher Perkins, et al.** *Curse of Strahd.* Wizards of the Coast, 2016.
A reworking of the Hickmans' 1983 module *Ravenloft*, widely considered the strongest adventure module of D&D 5th edition. Cited in Chapter 10 as an example of a well-designed adventure module: a self-contained aggregate with a clear interface to the surrounding campaign. The original *Ravenloft* established the gothic horror template the 5th edition version enriches.

**Hickman, Tracy, and Laura Hickman.** *Ravenloft.* TSR, 1983.
The original standalone module that introduced Strahd von Zarovich and the gothic horror register to D&D. Cited in Chapter 10 alongside *Curse of Strahd* as the source material for the 2016 5th edition adaptation.

**Dever, Joe.** *Lone Wolf* series. Berkley/Puffin, 1984–1998 (original series); further volumes published by Mongoose Publishing. Digital editions available at Project Aon: [projectaon.org](https://www.projectaon.org/).
A gamebook series spanning twenty-eight original volumes plus a sequel series, tracking a single character across decades of in-world time. Cited in Chapter 2 as the outer limit of what a gamebook series can attempt in terms of persistent state.

**Gygax, Gary, and Jeff Perren.** *Chainmail.* Guidon Games, 1971.
A medieval miniatures wargame whose Fantasy Supplement introduced monsters, heroes, and spells, and whose dice conventions were inherited directly by D&D. Cited in Chapter 6 as the ancestor of the d20 resolution system.

**Infocom.** *Zork.* 1977 (MIT); commercially released by Infocom, 1980.
The text adventure that brought parser fiction to a wider audience and defined the form for the decade that followed. Cited in Chapter 2.

**Jackson, Steve, and Ian Livingstone.** *Fighting Fantasy* series. Puffin Books/Wizard Books, 1982–present. [fightingfantasy.com](https://www.fightingfantasy.com/).
The gamebook series that shaped a generation. The first book, *The Warlock of Firetop Mountain*, established the format: second-person narration, numbered passages, Skill and Stamina stats, and dice-driven combat. Cited throughout the book as a formal ancestor of Mt. Graphnor and as a recurring point of comparison for game mechanics. Steve Jackson (the British one) also wrote the *Sorcery!* tetralogy referenced separately.

**Jackson, Steve.** *Sorcery!* tetralogy (Books 1–4). Puffin Books, 1983–1985.
A four-volume *Fighting Fantasy* series tracking items, injuries, and choices across all four books. Cited in Chapter 2 as an example of cross-volume state persistence: a subgraph that connects to the next volume at defined transition points.

**Parker, Chris, and Todd Howard (designers).** *The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim.* Bethesda Game Studios, 2011.
Cited in Chapter 4 for its skill-based character model, in which character level is derived from accumulated skill experience rather than assigned base stats. Morrowind (2002), also from Bethesda, is cited in the same footnote for making the explicit dice-roll mechanics of earlier editions visible in a way that produced the famous "swing and miss at point-blank range" situation.

**Robertson, John.** *The Dark Room.* YouTube, 2010; live show adaptation continuing.
An interactive adventure built from interconnected YouTube videos, navigated by searching for the next clip. Cited in Chapter 2 as an example of a gamebook structure whose edges depended on infrastructure the author did not control, and which eventually broke when YouTube's search behaviour changed. Also an example of the form being attempted on a platform completely unsuited to it, which has a certain admirable quality.

**Starke, Spenser, and Rowan Hall.** *Daggerheart.* Darrington Press, 2024.
A tabletop RPG with a distinctive dual-die resolution mechanic (Hope die and Fear die) that produces a total, a binary success/failure, and a narrative register simultaneously. Cited in Chapters 4, 6, and 7 for its character model, probability distribution, and asymmetric combat design respectively.

**Wizards of the Coast.** *Dungeons & Dragons System Reference Document 5.1.* 2023. Available under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International at [media.wizards.com/2023/downloads/dnd/SRD_CC_v5.1.pdf](https://media.wizards.com/2023/downloads/dnd/SRD_CC_v5.1.pdf).
The open rules reference used throughout this book for ability scores, character classes, skills, conditions, dice mechanics, equipment, and the proficiency bonus progression. Attribution is required and provided. The SRD is not the *Player's Handbook*; it is the subset of rules released for open use.

### Gamebook and Interactive Fiction Scholarship

**Borges, Jorge Luis.** "The Garden of Forking Paths." In *Ficciones*, 1941.
A short story built around branching paths, nested narratives, and an infinite garden of diverging time. Cited in Chapter 2 as an early literary deployment of branching-narrative structure, predating the gamebook form by forty years and the Netflix budget by considerably more.

**Lawford, Steve.** "Five-Room Dungeons." HAL open science, 2021. [enac.hal.science/hal-03097484/document](https://enac.hal.science/hal-03097484/document).
A graph-theoretic treatment of the Five Room Dungeon template, enumerating all twenty-one non-isomorphic connected digraphs on five vertices. Short, specific, and worth reading if you find yourself caring about this more than is strictly necessary. Cited in Chapter 2.

**Montfort, Nick.** *Twisty Little Passages: An Approach to Interactive Fiction.* MIT Press, 2003.
The definitive scholarly bridge between gamebook form, parser fiction, and the history of hypertext narrative. The title comes from a famously disorienting room description in *Colossal Cave Adventure*. Cited in Chapter 1 as the recommended starting point for readers who want to go deeper into the interactive fiction tradition. It will send you down a passage that has no guaranteed exit.

### Software Engineering

**Beck, Kent.** *Test-Driven Development: By Example.* Addison-Wesley, 2002.
The book that made test-first development a named practice rather than an instinct. The cycle of write a failing test, make it pass, refactor is Beck's; the application to domain logic with injectable dependencies in Chapter 14 is downstream of it. Cited in Chapter 14.

**Constantine, Larry, and Ed Yourdon.** *Structured Design: Fundamentals of a Discipline of Computer Program and Systems Design.* Prentice Hall, 1975.
The source of the cohesion and coupling vocabulary that still dominates software architecture discussion fifty years later. Cited in Chapter 10. The terminology has proliferated, been renamed, been rediscovered, and been argued about at length, but the underlying observation has held: things which change together should be together, and things that change independently should not know too much about each other.

**Evans, Eric.** *Domain-Driven Design: Tackling Complexity in the Heart of Software.* Addison-Wesley, 2003.
The blue book. Long, dense, and worth reading slowly. The core arguments about ubiquitous language, bounded contexts, aggregates, and repositories are clear; much of the bulk is examples and pattern catalogues. Martin Fowler's website ([martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/tags/domain%20driven%20design.html)) provides shorter, searchable treatments of the individual patterns. Cited in Chapter 13 as the named foundation for an approach most practitioners arrive at by instinct.

**Fielding, Roy T.** *Architectural Styles and the Design of Network-based Software Architectures.* Doctoral dissertation, University of California, Irvine, 2000. Chapter 5 available at [roy.gbiv.com/pubs/dissertation/rest_arch_style.htm](https://roy.gbiv.com/pubs/dissertation/rest_arch_style.htm).
The dissertation that defined REST and, in passing, gave HATEOAS its unwieldy name. Genuinely readable academic work, which is unusual for something that has generated this much conference-talk disagreement. Cited in Chapter 3.

**Fowler, Martin.** *Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture.* Addison-Wesley, 2002.
The source, among many other things, of the Post/Redirect/Get pattern cited in Chapter 3. Also a thorough taxonomy of the patterns that show up in data-heavy web applications (repositories, active records, table data gateways), most of which have been reinvented by every generation of web developers.

**Freeman, Steve, and Nat Pryce.** *Growing Object-Oriented Software, Guided by Tests.* Addison-Wesley, 2009.
The most practically useful book on TDD at the system level, applying the practice to designing interfaces and managing dependencies rather than just testing units in isolation. Cited in Chapter 14 as the recommended next step after Beck for readers who want to apply test-first thinking to a full application rather than individual functions.

**Gamma, Erich, Richard Helm, Ralph Johnson, and John Vlissides.** *Design Patterns: Elements of Reusable Object-Oriented Software.* Addison-Wesley, 1994.
The Gang of Four book. The source of "prefer composition over inheritance" and the adapter pattern. Cited in Chapters 5 and 10. Its specific patterns have dated unequally, but the underlying discipline of naming recurring solutions so they can be discussed is still the right instinct.

**Liskov, Barbara.** "Data Abstraction and Hierarchy." *SIGPLAN Notices*, vol. 23, no. 5, 1988 (keynote address, OOPSLA 1987).
The source of the Liskov Substitution Principle: a subtype should be substitutable for its parent type without altering the desirable properties of the program. Cited in Chapter 5. A foundational paper in object-oriented design that has been summarised, misunderstood, correctly understood, and cited selectively in approximately equal measure ever since.

**Martin, Robert C.** *Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design.* Prentice Hall, 2017.
The book that formalised the Dependency Inversion Principle as part of the SOLID set. Cited in Chapter 10. Martin's argument that domain logic should not depend on implementation details: the policy should not depend on the mechanism. This is the most practically useful single claim in the book.

**Parnas, David L.** "On the Criteria To Be Used in Decomposing Systems into Modules." *Communications of the ACM*, vol. 15, no. 12, December 1972.
A short paper that answered, permanently and elegantly, the question of how to divide software into modules. The answer: group things that change for the same reason, and hide the decisions that might need to change. Not "group things that are similar." Written in 1972 and has not required updating. Available through the ACM digital library. Cited in Chapter 10.

### Web Technologies

**Gross, Carson.** *Hypermedia Systems.* 2023. Available free online at [hypermedia.systems](https://hypermedia.systems/).
The companion book to htmx: a thorough, occasionally combative examination of how the web drifted away from its hypermedia roots and how to drift back. The case for HTML as a sufficient medium for most web interactions. Required reading if any part of Chapter 3 makes you want to argue about JavaScript frameworks.

**Nelson, Ted.** "A File Structure for the Complex, the Changing and the Indeterminate." *ACM National Conference*, 1965.
The paper that coined the word "hypertext." Nelson's vision for a global hypertext system called Xanadu, which began in 1960 and has been "almost finished" for most of the subsequent six decades. The web is a simpler system than Nelson had in mind. It works, which Xanadu largely has not, and Nelson has had strong feelings about this for some time. Cited in Chapter 3.

### Game Design Theory

**Elias, George Skaff, Richard Garfield, and K. Robert Gutschera.** *Characteristics of Games.* MIT Press, 2012.
A rigorous analytical treatment of what makes games work as systems. Cited in Chapter 6 alongside Schell for its more analytical approach to probability, variance, and the relationship between skill and luck in game design. If Schell is the book you give a designer, Elias et al. is the book you give someone who wants to understand why the designer made those choices.

**Schell, Jesse.** *The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses.* Taylor & Francis, 2008 (third edition 2019).
A wide-ranging treatment of game design practice, including a useful section on risk, reward, and the emotional geometry of chance. Cited in Chapter 6 in the context of why dice feel the way they do, which is a question that has no clean answer but several illuminating ways of looking at it.

## Part Two: Works Consulted

The following influenced the thinking behind this book without being directly cited or quoted. The honest record of what was read, played, and argued with during the years this accumulated.

**Bloch, Joshua.** *Effective Java.* Third edition. Addison-Wesley, 2018.
Primarily a Java book, but the advice on API design (minimise the public surface; every export is a commitment) is language-agnostic and correct. Mentioned obliquely in Chapter 10 in the context of minimal module interfaces.

**Dever, Joe.** *Kai Grand Master* series (*Lone Wolf* books 13–20). Especially *The Plague Lords of Ruel* and *The Dungeons of Torgar.* Berkley, 1991–1993.
The books in the *Lone Wolf* series where the campaign graph becomes genuinely complex, with branching routes through specific cities and dungeons that have meaningfully different consequences. A practical example of what a production-scale gamebook graph looks like.

**Fowler, Martin.** *Refactoring: Improving the Design of Existing Code.* Addison-Wesley, 1999 (second edition 2018).
The book that named and catalogued the refactoring practice. The core argument, change structure without changing behaviour and rely on tests to verify the preservation, underpins Chapter 10 directly.

**Humble, Jez, and David Farley.** *Continuous Delivery: Reliable Software Releases through Build, Deployment, and Test Automation.* Addison-Wesley, 2010.
The book that systematised the deployment pipeline. Chapter 14's verification manifest is a scaled-down version of the pipeline thinking here: a sequence of gates, each catching a different class of failure, with the whole chain providing confidence that what was built is what was shipped.

**Jackson, Steve (the American one).** *Car Wars.* Steve Jackson Games, 1981.
A vehicular combat game with an unusually clear rules structure. Not directly cited, but a reminder that good game design is essentially the same discipline as good domain modelling.

**Metz, Sandi.** *Practical Object-Oriented Design: An Agile Primer Using Ruby.* Addison-Wesley, 2012 (second edition 2018).
The clearest practical treatment of composition over inheritance and the single responsibility principle, using Ruby but applicable to any object-oriented language. The arguments in Chapter 5 about when inheritance is right and when composition is better are downstream of thinking in this book.

**Schwaber, Ken, and Jeff Sutherland.** *The Scrum Guide.* 2020. [scrumguides.org](https://www.scrumguides.org/).
Cited in passing in the delivery practice context behind Chapter 14's acceptance notes. The sprint review's requirement that working software be demonstrated rather than asserted is the same instinct as "assertions with receipts."

## Part Three: Further Reading

For the reader who has finished this book and would like to descend further. Each entry includes a note on what it offers and why it is worth the time.

---

**Berners-Lee, Tim, James Hendler, and Ora Lassila.** "The Semantic Web." *Scientific American*, May 2001.
The original vision for linked data and machine-readable provenance on the web. The rules-provenance work in Chapter 11 is a tiny, practical instance of the larger question this article asks: what does it mean for data to carry its own meaning and origin with it? The vision was ambitious; the practice has been uneven; the question remains useful.

**Date, C.J.** *An Introduction to Database Systems.* Eighth edition. Pearson, 2003.
The canonical relational database textbook. The normalisation arguments in Chapter 11 (store facts once, reference them everywhere) and Chapter 12's discussion of the difference between stored and derived facts both have deep foundations here. Not light reading, but definitive.

**Evans, Eric.** *Domain-Driven Design: Tackling Complexity in the Heart of Software.* Addison-Wesley, 2003.
Listed again here because Chapter 13 is an introduction, not a substitute. The pattern catalogues for repositories, domain services, factories, and sagas are worth working through properly if you find yourself building systems with genuinely complex domain logic. Martin Fowler's summary articles are a good warm-up.

**Hunt, Andrew, and David Thomas.** *The Pragmatic Programmer: From Journeyman to Master.* Addison-Wesley, 1999 (twentieth anniversary edition 2019).
The book that introduced DRY as a named principle, among many other things. Practical, opinionated, and still accurate. The advice to "keep knowledge in one place" and "make the machine do the work" is the practical form of everything Chapter 11 says about generated attribution panels and stable identifiers.

**Montfort, Nick.** *Twisty Little Passages: An Approach to Interactive Fiction.* MIT Press, 2003.
Listed again in further reading because Chapter 2's treatment of gamebook history is necessarily brief. Montfort covers Colossal Cave, Zork, the parser tradition, the hypertext fiction movement, and the relationship between all of them in the kind of depth that deserves its own reading session. The interactive fiction canon is richer and stranger than the Fighting Fantasy shelf suggests.

**Newman, Sam.** *Building Microservices: Designing Fine-Grained Systems.* O'Reilly, 2015 (second edition 2021).
The practical treatment of bounded contexts and service boundaries at the architecture level. Chapter 13's bounded context discussion is the within-a-monolith version of the problems Newman addresses at the service level. The vocabulary is the same; the blast radius of a wrong boundary decision is larger.

**Shore, James, and Shane Warden.** *The Art of Agile Development.* O'Reilly, 2007 (second edition 2021).
The delivery chapters informed the acceptance note practice in Chapter 14. The argument that delivered software requires demonstration, documentation, and deferred work to be named rather than claimed to be complete, is the honest version of shipping.

**Vernon, Vaughn.** *Implementing Domain-Driven Design.* Addison-Wesley, 2013.
The companion volume to Evans that covers the implementation details Evans left underspecified: how to implement aggregates in code, how to handle domain events, how to structure repositories. If Chapter 13 left you wanting the practical layer below the patterns, this is the book.

The presence of a work does not imply endorsement of everything in it. The further reading annotations reflect personal judgement, not editorial neutrality.*
