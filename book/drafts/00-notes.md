## Notes on the Drafts
- The introduction needs to explain more why I'm building a hypertext gamebook to explain softtware. Are text adventures dead? Not exactly, but certainly less popular. Text adventures were some of the earliest games due to technical limitations, but they really showed off some of the complex processing and node chaining a computer could do that a fighting fantasy adventure couldn't. The text adventure game, or hyper-text in our case, is a brilliant example of what software can do. Also, I'me a nerd and I grew up with FF before graduating to D&D and computer games, so as I approach middle age I believe I am allowed some nostalgia - it's certainly better than wasting money on a sports car and committing adultery.
- the code samples should be written in a 'clean' nd simple manner - no nested ternaries, no magic numbers, no fancy language specific tricks unless they are called out and explained. It should be written so that anyone with some knowledge of code, irrespective of language, could easily understand hat it is doing.
- any reproduced tests should follow the Arrange, Act & Assert comment heading pattern to make them even more intuitive to non-programmers.
- has the audience been considered in all this? I am writing for a reasonably intelligent readership, not necessarily familiar with both programming and role-playing games, but maybe (and if stereotypes are to be believed) both. The roleplaying concepts should be explained as much as the coding ones. Don't have to spend paragraphs explaining what a variable is or what spell levels are, but give the headline so a reader can grasp enough of the idea to understand it in context.
- does the stack being used need mentioning in introduction? I explain that while typescript might no be the 'best' language, hono maybe not the best server framework etc, jsx not the best templating language etc they are the tools I know best, and more often than not what 'best' actual mean in software development practically is the set of tools the majority of the team are comfortable with. Also it is web based, and this is a web project.
- the language used by characters in later dialogues is too literal and meta. Concepts like 'save', game design, a testing party etc would not be known to these people. The excerpts are allegorical - the characters must be discussing things that are similar to the concepts explored in the chapter, not the actual things themselves. As long as the two can map onto each other it is fine - and if the mapping is really silly, that could be called out as a joke once or twice.
- be good in the bit about http status codes to explain status codes further in a footnote, explaining that no one uses 100s, 200s are good, 30s something else, 400 your bad, 500 my bad. Also link to the best status code: I'm a teapot.
- check for over use of formulaic constructions in the prose. A few here and there are fine, but I'm seeing patterns. For example, there's  quite a few short one sentence paagraphs, a lot of 'not an x, but a y'
- I wanted to emphasise the concept of domain driven design more, as this book is basically an exploration of domain driven design for roleplaying games. It is mentioned briefly in earlier chapters but the thread is not picked up again later. It could be a whole chapter, the last one before the conclusion that ties up what we've done and give it a name.
- not sure if chapter 13 is worth it. It's all about a very meta process for the reader. A bit too much of a look behind the curtain of the game book. This book should be using the gamebook explorations in a way that the user would be able to see examples of hat is discussed when they play the finished full gamebook accompanying this book. The author tooling can still be mentioned in passing in the roles based chapter, I just don't think it needs a chapter of it's own.
- bear in mind that mt. graphnor is not fully finished - it will be improved to include a pokemon/jrpg like battle menu/system and full fledged nodes. I will be redrafting the book once I've finished it's development, so you don't need to write speculatively about these features, but perhaps lay off talking about nit's future development. The book should treat it as a finished product.
- the links between rpgs and software fade in later chapters, which might be expected due to their nature, but ideally each chapter should focus on software but also mention stuff from the rpg/fantasy/video game world too, either using it as an analogy where appropriate (or particularly funny) or in sme other way that makes sense in context.
- For all terms introduced or used there should be a glossary in the appendix. It should explain all the terms simply, and occasionally with some humour akin to dr. johnson's dictionary. The occasional joke should not come at the expense of understanding the concept though.
- the appendix should also include a full bibliography for all references mentioned, used during research but not quoted and also further reading for the true nerds
